Graph generation, text parsing, priority-queue construction, and the initial
allocation of the distance array are outside the timing loop. Reinitialization of
the distance array remains inside each timed search. The local in-queue marker
array for SLF+LLL is allocated in its search function and is included in the timing.
The random-graph and complexity curves use CPU time per invocation; the error bars
show the \textbf{sample standard deviation} over three repetitions, not confidence
intervals or the dispersion of the 1,000 queries. The road-network table uses
wall-clock time in the search loop divided by the number of queries. The raw CSV
also retains both CPU and wall-clock times for the complete batch.

\subsection{USA Road Network Inputs and Results}
The NY graph has $n=264346$ and $m=733846$, while the BAY graph has $n=321270$ and
$m=800172$. Both graphs use the complete set of 1,000 source-target queries. Each
implementation runs one complete batch, for a total of $9\times2\times1000$ searches.
The road-network data come from the 9th DIMACS Implementation Challenge
\cite{dimacs}.

On NY and BAY, the radix heap achieves speedups of approximately 1.57 and 1.48 over
the standard-library queue, respectively. SLF+LLL achieves speedups of 2.73 and
2.22, while the Fibonacci heap takes 2.26 and 2.49 times as long as the
standard-library queue. FIFO SPFA requires 249.409 and 353.306 ms/query and is much
slower.
These results apply only to the present inputs; they do not imply that FIFO SPFA is
always slow or that SLF+LLL always has an advantage.

\subsection{Random Graph Parameter Sweep}
The random directed graphs contain 19 $(n,m)$ configurations, with nine
implementations per configuration, for a total of 171 cases. Multiedges are allowed,
self-loops are excluded, weights lie in $[0,200000]$, and the source is vertex 1.

\subsection{Fixed-Density Experiments and Complexity Fitting}
The fixed-density experiments use two graph families: a sparse family with $m=4n$
and $n=2^{10},\ldots,2^{17}$, and a dense family with $m=n(n-1)/4$ and
$n=2^7,\ldots,2^{12}$. Nine implementations are measured at every size in each
family, for a total of $9(8+6)=126$ cases.
\plotfigure{complexity-sparse}{Mean CPU time for the sparse family, $m=4n$. Error bars show the sample standard deviation over three repetitions.}{fig:sparse}
\plotfigure{complexity-dense}{Mean CPU time for the dense family, $m=n(n-1)/4$. Error bars show the sample standard deviation over three repetitions.}{fig:dense}

Google Benchmark fits the models $f(n)=n\log_2n$ and $f(n)=n^2$ using the
origin-constrained least-squares form $T(n)\approx c f(n)$. In Table~\ref{tab:fits},
the sparse coefficients are measured in ns/$(n\log_2n)$ and the dense coefficients
in ns/$n^2$; coefficients from the two columns cannot be compared directly. RMS is
the normalized residual percentage defined by Google Benchmark and measures how well
the model fits within the observed range.

Applying the same model to SPFA is an empirical fit and is not used to infer its
asymptotic bound. For dense graphs, the theoretical term for the Fibonacci heap is
$m+n\log n=\Theta(n^2)$, whereas the usual upper bound for heap-based Dijkstra
contains $m\log n$. At finite sizes, edge scanning may still dominate timing, so a
good $n^2$ fit does not imply that the logarithmic factor has disappeared.
